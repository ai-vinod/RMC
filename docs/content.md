## Rules for every line on the site

These apply to all copy, everywhere, including the blog. They exist because medical advertising in India is regulated and because the clinic's credibility is the thing being sold.

**Never write:**

- Superlatives — best, finest, leading, number one, most advanced
- Cure claims — cures, eliminates, permanent solution, guaranteed results
- Outcome promises — pain-free in a week, 100% success, you will be walking in three days
- Comparisons with other clinics or doctors
- Equipment or drug brand names
- Before-and-after photographs
- Prices for any treatment
- Anything about procedures the clinic does not actually perform

**Always write:**

- Plain words a patient would use, not medical vocabulary. "Hair fall", not "androgenetic alopecia" — though the medical term can appear once, in brackets
- Short sentences. Under 25 words
- Specifics instead of adjectives. "Forty years of practice" beats "highly experienced"
- Qualifications exactly as given, never paraphrased or expanded
- What the patient can expect, rather than what the clinic offers

**Tone:** warm, plain, and calm. The way a good doctor explains something in the consulting room. Not clinical, not corporate, not salesy.

**A test for any sentence:** would Dr. Madhavan be comfortable if a patient quoted it back to him? If not, cut it.

### Notes on the choices

**Page title.** Version A wins on search, and it is not close. Nobody in Kanchipuram is searching "Rani Multi Speciality Clinic" yet, so a brand-first title spends the most valuable 60 characters on a term with no demand. Revisit in a year.

**The clinic dates from 1985** — confirmed 4 Oct. It was rebranded as Rani Multi Speciality Clinic in 2015, but the practice has run since 1985. Dr. Paramanantham's forty years of experience sits alongside that, not instead of it.

That makes a better line available than "four decades", because a date is checkable and a round number is not:

> Caring for Kanchipuram since 1985

Use it in the hero, or as the third reason to choose. It is the single strongest sentence available for this clinic — no competitor in Kanchipuram can claim it, it needs no qualifier, and it does the work that "trusted" and "experienced" fail to do. Worth putting somewhere a patient sees in the first five seconds.

**What is deliberately absent.** No "best", "leading", "advanced" or "state of the art". No claim about outcomes. No prices. No equipment names. Every line above would survive a patient quoting it back across the consulting room desk.

**Review count.** Written as "more than 130" rather than a fixed number, so it does not go stale.

**Still to write for this page:** the three doctor cards reuse the names, qualifications and roles already settled in the requirements document. Nothing new needed there.

**No patient video section** — dropped from launch on 4 Oct, so that field has gone from this table.

**Two rules for article drafts.**

First, nothing clinic-specific gets invented. Not recovery times, not costs, not how long a procedure takes at this clinic, not the protocol followed. Where a specific is needed, leave a blank for the doctor to fill. A doctor skimming a draft will approve a plausible-sounding sentence without checking it, and that is how a wrong number ends up published under his name.

Second, the visit section and the what-to-try-first section are where most clinic blogs are thin, and they are the two that actually bring people through the door. Give them the most attention.

## Sending it to the doctors

This is the longest wait on the project. Four doctors, one of them out of town, and medical claims that need sign-off. Start it before the build is finished, not after.

**Send once, not in pieces.** A single document with everything is one review. Dribbling sections out over a fortnight is four reviews, and each one restarts the waiting.

**Send it to Dr. Madhavan only.** He is the single approver. He collects the others' input. Four separate approval threads is how a week becomes a month.

**Ask for three specific things**, because "please review" gets a reply of "looks good" that is worth nothing:

1. Anything factually wrong — particularly qualifications, conditions treated, and treatments listed
2. Anything the clinic does not actually do
3. The blanks filled, which are marked and are the things only a doctor can answer

**Give a date.** "By Friday" gets a reply. "When you get a chance" does not.

**A message that works:**

> Doctor, here is the full text for the website — all pages in one document. Three things I need from you:
>
> 1. Tell me anything that is factually wrong, especially the qualifications and the list of what each doctor treats.
> 2. Tell me if anything listed is not something the clinic actually does. I have left out prices and avoided any claims about results.
> 3. There are a few blanks marked in yellow — those are things only you can answer, like what happens at a first visit.
>
> If you can send it back by \[date\] we stay on the four-week timeline. Happy to go through it on a call instead if that is quicker.

**Two items to raise separately**, because they are decisions rather than corrections:

- **The robotic joint replacement question.** Where do those procedures actually take place? Needed before the ortho page is written.
- **His display name.** "Dr. P Madhavan" or "Dr. Paramanantham Madhavan"? His father's entry sits directly above his on the About page and they share a first name.

**What comes back is content, not design.** If he comments on colours or layout at this stage, note it and park it — the design conversation happens against a built page, not a text document. Mixing the two is how approval loops stretch.
